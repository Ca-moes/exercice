What core training should prioritise (and how strong the evidence is)

Low-back health

  • Exercise helps chronic low back pain, but no single type wins.
    • Cochrane 2021 (249 RCTs): moderate-certainty evidence that exercise reduces pain by about 15/100 compared with no treatment. The effect on function is small.
    • Cochrane 2016 on motor-control ("core stability") exercise: low to high quality evidence that it is not clinically better than other exercise.
    • Takeaway: core work is useful, but nothing about it is magic. Consistency and progressive loading matter more than picking the "right" exercise.
  • Trunk endurance matters more than maximal strength.
    • Biering-Sørensen (1984, n≈930): in men, good isometric back-extensor endurance predicted fewer first-time low back episodes.
    • McGill (1999) published normal endurance ratios. In healthy men, side-bridge time was about 65% of extensor time and about 99% of flexor time.
    • McGill's framework, as summarised by ACE: build endurance with short holds of about 8–10 s in a reverse pyramid of reps, keeping the spine in neutral.
    • Evidence quality: prospective cohort data plus expert and biomechanical reasoning. Moderate at best.
  • Train the core in every direction instead of hunting for one muscle.
    • Kavcic et al. (2004): no single muscle dominated spine stability, and each muscle's role changed with the task. That supports covering anti-extension, anti-lateral-flexion, anti-rotation and extensors rather than targeting "the TVA" or "the lower abs".
    • Axler & McGill (1997): no single abdominal exercise trains all the abdominal muscles at a low spine cost, so you need a variety. Partial curl-ups had the best challenge-to-spine-load ratio.

Isometric vs dynamic, endurance vs strength

  • Anti-movement work should be the base.
    • Isometric holds and slow "resist the movement" drills (plank or body saw, side plank, Pallof press, bird dog, carries) give high abdominal activation with low spine load.
    • Callaghan & McGill (2015): the body saw produced substantial abdominal activation in the most spine-conserving way of the exercises tested (<2500 N compression). The hanging straight-leg raise gave the highest activation (>130% MVC rectus abdominis) but about 3000 N of compression.
  • Add one dynamic exercise in the high-activation range. Escamilla et al. (2006, 2010) found hanging knee-ups (with straps), the 30° incline reverse crunch, and ab-wheel/Swiss-ball roll-outs and pikes activated the abdominals the most.
  • Practical split: short holds and endurance work (McGill style) for spine health, plus one or two harder exercises at roughly 8–12 reps for strength and capacity.
  • Ekstrom et al. (2007) found the side bridge is strong enough to strengthen gluteus medius and external oblique, and the quadruped arm/leg lift (bird dog) is strong enough for gluteus maximus. Other bodyweight core drills fell below 45% MVIC, so they mostly train endurance.

Sit-ups and crunches

  • Not needed, and a poor fit for him.
    • Escamilla (2006): the bent-knee sit-up and the inclined reverse crunch drive high rectus femoris (hip flexor) activity. The authors flagged this as possibly problematic for people with back problems. For someone with tight hip flexors and APT, hip-flexor-dominant sit-ups train the wrong thing.
    • Axler & McGill (1997): partial curl-ups gave the best muscle challenge for the spine load.
  • Honest caveat: there is no good evidence that occasional crunches injure healthy people. McGill's concern about repeated flexion comes mainly from animal-spine lab models. Crunches are simply low value here.

APT and lordosis (manage expectations)

  • Ab strength does not explain pelvic tilt. Walker et al. (1987) found almost no correlation between abdominal performance and either pelvic tilt (rho=.18) or lordosis (rho=.06) in standing.
  • Core training will not "fix" APT on its own. Posture is mostly habit plus the mobility work he already does.
  • A posterior-tilt bias is still a reasonable coaching choice.
    • It teaches lumbo-pelvic control.
    • It raises external-oblique activity: Schoenfeld et al. (2014) found a long-lever plank with posterior tilt beat a standard plank for upper rectus abdominis, lower stabilisers and external oblique.
    • It keeps hip flexors from taking over.

Running

  • Core-specific evidence is weak.
    • Sato & Mokha (2009, n=20 finished): 6 weeks of core training gave faster 5000 m times but no change in ground reaction forces or lower-limb stability.
    • Hung et al. (2019, n=21): 8 weeks at 3 sessions/week lowered VO2 only at the fastest stage.
    • Stanton et al. (2004): Swiss-ball core training improved core stability but not running economy.
    • Prieske/Dong et al. meta-analysis (2023): core training helps balance and jumping (moderate GRADE). Running-economy evidence is thin.
  • General strength training has better running-economy evidence. Balsalobre-Fernández (2016) found a large effect of lower-body strength plus plyometrics in trained runners. His lifting days matter more for running than the core day does.
  • What the core day can do for running: frontal-plane control (side plank, suitcase carry, single-leg work) and rotation control (Pallof). These are mechanistically plausible but not proven.

Dosing

  • Endurance holds (McGill style):
    • Hold 8–10 s per rep, in a reverse pyramid such as 6-4-2 (ACE) or 5-3-1 (Squat University).
    • Rest 20–30 s between sets.
    • Add reps over time; don't lengthen the holds.
  • Anti-movement and dynamic strength:
    • 2–3 sets of 6–12 reps, or holds of 20–40 s.
    • Rest 30–60 s.
    • Stop 1–2 reps short of form breaking (the lower back arches, or the pelvis tips forward).
  • Is once a week enough?
    • For maintaining and modest gains, yes. Spiering et al. (2021, JSCR) found strength can be maintained for up to 32 weeks with 1 session per week and 1 set per exercise, provided intensity stays high.
    • The core-training studies that showed effects mostly used 2–5 sessions per week for 6–8 weeks, so one session a week will progress more slowly.
    • He already gets extra frequency from:
      • the daily dead bug (anti-extension, already in the postural routine),
      • pull-ups and dips on the tower (bracing), and
      • his lifting days.
    • If he wants faster progress: add a 3-minute "mini-core" after one run (one side plank and one bird dog set). That's optional.
  • Progression rule: when he hits the top of the rep or hold range on every set with clean form, move that exercise one step along its progression column.

Design constraints applied

  • No dead bug: it's already done daily. The session covers its gaps instead: lateral, rotational and posterior work, plus a harder anti-extension exercise.
  • Posterior-tilt bias: every front-core exercise uses a "ribs down, tailbone tucked" cue. No hip-flexor-dominant sit-ups or straight-leg raises yet.
  • Groin: no Copenhagen in the base session. Side planks start on the knees or with feet staggered. Body saw and knee raise have no adductor load.
  • Running: side plank and Pallof cover frontal and transverse control. The suitcase carry is the swap when a heavy backpack is available.

Evidence quality summary (honest)

  • Moderate: exercise helps low back pain overall (Cochrane 2021), and no exercise type is clearly superior (Cochrane 2016).
  • Moderate (cohort): back-extensor endurance predicts fewer first-time low back episodes in men (Biering-Sørensen).
  • Lab/EMG and modelling only: exercise rankings by muscle activation and spine load (Callaghan & McGill 2015, Escamilla 2006/2010, Ekstrom 2007, Axler & McGill 1997, Schoenfeld 2014). These are small samples of healthy young people. Activation is not the same as training outcome.
  • Weak: core training improving running performance or economy (small RCTs with mixed results).
  • Weak for him: the Pallof press has no outcome trials, and the anti-rotation rationale is expert consensus. The Copenhagen evidence is strong for injury prevention in footballers and promising for groin-pain rehab, but those were male athletes.
  • Contradicts a common assumption: abdominal strength does not correlate with APT (Walker 1987). The posterior-tilt cueing is a reasonable coaching choice, not a proven fix.
